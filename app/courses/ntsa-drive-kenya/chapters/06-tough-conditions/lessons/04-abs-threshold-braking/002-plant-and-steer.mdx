ABS, the Anti-lock Braking System, lives in almost every modern matatu, Probox, and saloon car on Kenyan roads from about 2010 onward. It does one job: it stops your wheels from locking under emergency braking, so you can still steer.

A locked wheel does not roll. It skids. A skidding tyre has almost no grip and zero ability to steer. ABS keeps each wheel just barely rotating at the threshold of lock-up, hundreds of times a minute, by releasing and reapplying brake pressure in microbursts. The result: shorter stopping distances on most surfaces, and a car that still goes where you point it.

The technique to use it is one phrase.

<Rule>
  Plant and steer. In an ABS emergency stop, press the brake pedal as hard as
  you can and keep it pressed. Do not lift, do not pump. Then steer around the
  obstacle. Both inputs at once.
</Rule>

Plant means: full force on the pedal, instantly, and hold it. The pedal will pulse, buzz, or vibrate, and the system may make a grinding noise. That is the ABS module working. Lifting off because it feels strange undoes the system's whole job.

Steer means: once you have planted the brake, you can and should steer around the obstacle. This is the trick that pre-ABS drivers find counterintuitive: in an older car, full braking and steering at the same time would have lost the rear in an instant. With ABS, the two are compatible.

<Callout type="tip">
  An ABS warning light glowing yellow on the dashboard while you drive means the
  system has faulted. The brakes still work, but they work like a non-ABS car.
  Switch to threshold-braking technique until it is repaired.
</Callout>

ABS is now standard on most vehicles sold new in Kenya. But many older saloons, lorries, and rural pickups have no ABS at all. That is where threshold braking comes in.
