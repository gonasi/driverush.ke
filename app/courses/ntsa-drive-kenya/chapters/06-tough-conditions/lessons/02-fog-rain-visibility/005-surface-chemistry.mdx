You now know the headline rule: first rain is the dangerous rain. Here is the why behind it, so you remember it under pressure.

Every dry day, Kenyan tarmac quietly collects three things. Diesel leaks from heavy vehicles. Worn rubber from countless tyres. Brake dust and engine oil from older matatus and trucks. The hotter and drier the spell, the thicker the film.

The first sprinkle of rain does not wash that mix away. Water is heavier than oil, so it slides under the film and lifts it just clear of the tarmac. For about ten minutes you are not driving on wet road, you are driving on a layer of oily emulsion. Grip can fall by half compared to dry tarmac.

<Rule>
  When the first drops hit the windscreen, drop your speed by at least 20 km/h
  and double your following distance. Hold that until the rain has been steady
  for ten minutes or the road surface looks clean and wet.
</Rule>

This is also why long-dry roads after weeks without rain, like the Thika superhighway in February or the Mombasa Road approaches in dry season, are far more dangerous in the first downpour than a road that gets rained on weekly.

<Callout type="tip">
  Watch the spray off the vehicle ahead. Heavy white spray means the road is
  already flushed and grippy-wet. Almost no spray with the road looking
  greasy-shiny means the film is still up. Slow down more.
</Callout>
